Herding Ball for Corgis: Which Size to Buy and How to Get Your Corgi to Play With It

Every Pembroke Welsh Corgi has the instinct to herd, but that does not mean a herding ball will make sense the second you roll it across the floor. Between the size of the ball, the surface you use it on, and your dog's willingness to push rather than nip, there is more to getting it right than people expect.

Home›Blog›Herding Ball for Corgis

Which herding ball size works for a Pembroke Welsh Corgi? Most adult Pembrokes do best with a 10-inch herding ball — roughly chest-height for a 10-12 inch tall dog. An 8-inch ball works for puppies and compact adults, and a 12-inch ball is usable in loose soil or on a slope but often frustrating indoors. The specific product you choose matters less than getting the size and the introduction sequence right.

A herding ball — sometimes called a push ball, treatball ball, or urban herding ball — is essentially a large, heavy exercise ball that a dog pushes with its nose or chest. The sport of treatball was invented in Germany in 2005 as a way to keep herding dogs active when livestock was not available. Because the Pembroke is a herding breed at its core, the activity taps into instincts most corgis already carry. The trick is to present the ball in a way that makes those instincts click.

The corgi owner's challenge is that the same instinct that makes a Pembroke chase and circle also makes it try to nip the ball. A Cardigan Welsh Corgi owner on YouTube documented this exact moment: their puppy, Hoagie, approached a new herding ball, poked it, then immediately started biting the grass around it instead of pushing. Why size is the first and most important question

A herding ball that is too small encourages the corgi to pick it up or bite it. One that is too large is physically hard to move. The right size sits between the corgi's elbow and shoulder when placed next to the dog. For a Pembroke that stands 10-12 inches at the shoulder, that generally works out as follows:

8 inches3-4 lbPuppies, compact adults under 10 inLight enough for a young dog to push; some adults pick it up instead of pushing

The ball weight matters almost as much as the diameter. A 10-inch vinyl herding ball weighs about 5-7 lb, which is enough to give satisfying resistance when the dog pushes but light enough that a 25-30 lb corgi can move it across the yard. The inflatable kind weighs less but also bounces erratically, which some corgis love and some find startling.

Hard plastic vs. inflatable: what works for a corgi

Herding balls come in two main constructions, and the choice matters for a breed with a long back and a tendency to nip.

Hard plastic (Jolly Ball style): These are one-piece, hollow, hard plastic balls with a handle. They weigh more, roll in a straight line, and resist punctures. The handle gives the dog something else to interact with, which some corgis prefer to nose-pushing. The downside: on hardwood or tile they are loud, and a corgi that tries to pick up a 10-inch hard ball by the handle can sling it into furniture.

Inflatable vinyl (Treibball style): These are smooth, inflatable balls used in competitive treibball. They bounce slightly, which makes movement more playful, and they are quieter indoors. The surface gives better nose traction than hard plastic. They are easier to puncture — a corgi that bites rather than pushes can leave teeth marks that turn into leaks.

For a first ball on a corgi that does not yet understand pushing, an inflatable 10-inch ball is usually the better choice because it is less intimidating. For a dog that already chases and bites, a hard plastic ball may last longer.

How treibball works and why it suits corgis

Treibball (German for "drive ball") is an organized dog sport where a handler directs a dog from a distance while the dog pushes large exercise balls into a goal. The sport originated in Germany in 2005 as an urban alternative to livestock herding. It was designed specifically for breeds like the Pembroke: dogs with strong herding instincts but no access to sheep.

In a competitive setting the balls are 45-75 cm (18-30 inches) in diameter — far larger than what a single corgi would push for play. But the principle is the same: the dog uses its nose and chest to apply directional pressure, and the handler guides with voice and hand signals from a stationary spot. That directional understanding is what a home herding ball session builds toward, even if you never enter a competition.

Step by step: introducing a herding ball to a corgi that has never seen one

A corgi's first reaction to a herding ball is often confusion, not instinct. The breed is bred to circle livestock and nip at heels — a silent, stationary ball does not trigger that sequence the way a moving animal would. The introduction is a teaching process, not a test.

  1. Let the corgi inspect the ball at rest. Place the ball on the floor or grass and let your dog sniff, paw, circle, and figure out that it is not a threat. Do not roll it yet. Some corgis bark at it for a minute and then ignore it — that is normal.
  2. Add movement away from the dog. Roll the ball slowly away from your corgi, not toward it. A ball rolling toward the dog can feel threatening. A ball rolling away triggers chase instinct.
  3. Reward any interaction. Click or treat for nose touches, paw taps, and especially for pushing with the nose or chest. If the corgi bites the ball, redirect by rolling it away again rather than scolding.
  4. Use a hallway or a fence line. A confined space keeps the ball from rolling too far and helps the corgi understand that pushing the ball in one direction is the goal. A 6-8 foot wide hallway or a fenced garden strip works well.
  5. Build duration slowly. Start with 3-5 minute sessions, twice a day. End while the corgi still shows interest — do not wait until it walks away, or the ball becomes a boring object.
  6. Introduce direction cues once the dog pushes reliably. Add a verbal cue like "push" or "ball" when the corgi moves the ball, then "stop" or "wait" when you want it to pause. These are the same directional concepts used in multi-dog household management when you need both dogs to settle.

The corgi in the video above took several sessions before pushing clicked. The owner reported that Hoagie spent the first two days mainly barking at the ball and biting the ground beside it — then on day three he nudged it by accident and chased it when it rolled, and from there the instinct took over.

Where to use it: surfaces that help and surfaces that hurt

The surface you choose has a bigger effect on success than most owners expect. A herding ball behaves differently on grass, dirt, concrete, and indoor flooring, and a corgi's experience changes with it.

For a first session, short grass or a bare earth patch in the yard is the ideal setup. If you live in an apartment without outdoor access, a long hallway on a yoga mat or a rug works — the key is enough length for the ball to roll at least 8-10 feet before hitting a wall.

Troubleshooting: what to do when your corgi will not push

"My corgi just barks at it."

That is the herding instinct expressing itself as frustration. The dog wants to move the thing but does not know how. Leave the ball stationary in the yard or on the living room floor for a day or two so the corgi acclimates. Often the barking stops when the ball becomes a familiar object.

"My corgi bites the ball instead of pushing it."

A persistent biter is treating the ball as a chew toy, not a herding target. Switch to a hard plastic ball that resists teeth or an inflatable ball that is fully deflated — a flat ball cannot be bitten, only nudged. Reinflate as the dog learns to push. This is the same redirecting approach used during the corgi biting phase, but applied to an object instead of a person.

"My corgi pushes the ball once and then ignores it."

The ball likely rolled too fast and the dog did not understand what happened. Slow the roll by starting on grass and pushing the ball gently. Some corgis do better with a ball that has slight resistance — an under-inflated vinyl ball drags slightly and gives more feedback with each nudge.

"My corgi is scared of the ball."

If the corgi backs away, tucks its tail, or refuses to approach, the ball is either too large or too noisy. Size down to an 8-inch inflatable and place it on carpet or grass to eliminate noise. Let the dog investigate at its own pace — do not roll the ball toward it.

What to watch for: herding ball safety for corgis

A corgi's long back means certain movements matter more than they would for a dog with a shorter spine. Pushing a ball is generally low-impact, but a few precautions keep it safe:

  • Watch for overexertion. Corgis are surprisingly fast on short legs. A ten-minute ball session burns a lot of energy. Stop before the dog lies down or pants heavily — this is especially true for older corgis or those managing their weight, as discussed in our corgi weight guide.
  • Check the ball for damage. Inflatable balls develop slow leaks where teeth have scored the surface. Hard plastic balls can crack at the seam after rough use on concrete. Replace or deflate damaged balls.
  • Use on a soft surface for beginners. Concrete or asphalt can scrape a corgi's nose if it pushes too enthusiastically. Grass, dirt, or a rubber mat protects the face and chest.
  • Do not chase the ball yourself. The game should be the dog moving the ball, not you kicking it and the dog chasing. If you find yourself doing all the work, go back to stationary introduction and reward only pushes.
  • Supervise any new ball until you know how your corgi interacts with it. Some corgis are destroyers who will deflate a vinyl ball in minutes. Others treat it gently. Learn which kind you have before leaving the ball out unattended.

How long it takes for a corgi to learn treibball basics

From the owner reports collected across forums and video channels, a Pembroke that has daily access to a correctly sized ball usually figures out intentional pushing within one to three sessions of 5-10 minutes each. The timeline looks roughly like this:

  • Session 1: Inspection, barking, maybe one accidental nudge. The dog may ignore the ball for long stretches.
  • Sessions 2-3: Purposeful nosing and chest pushes begin. The ball rolls a few feet before the dog loses interest.
  • Week 1: The corgi understands that pushing the ball makes it move and starts chasing after it.
  • Week 2-3: Directional awareness emerges. The dog begins to steer the ball rather than just bat it.

Not every corgi reaches the directional stage. Some are content to push the ball around the yard without any herding sequence, and that is a perfectly good outcome — the dog is exercising both its body and its herding brain.

FAQ

What size herding ball should I get for my corgi?

Most adult Pembrokes do best with a 10-inch herding ball (about 5-7 lb). The ball should reach roughly chest height when your corgi stands next to it.

Will a corgi naturally know how to push a herding ball?

Not automatically. Some corgis need encouragement, especially at first. The instinct is there, but the corgi may need to learn that pushing a ball works differently from nipping at heels.

Is a herding ball safe for corgi puppies?

Under supervision, yes. Use a smaller inflatable ball (8-inch or soft 10-inch) and keep sessions short to avoid frustration.

Can a corgi pop or damage a herding ball?

Nose-nippers and persistent biters can puncture inflatable balls over time. Hard plastic or heavy-duty vinyl balls last longer. Check the ball regularly for cracks or deflated spots.

Where should I use a herding ball with my corgi?

Outdoors on grass or an indoor hallway with enough room to roll. Avoid concrete: a heavy ball skids too fast and can discourage a new player.

How long should a herding ball session last for a corgi?

Start with 5-10 minutes. Corgis are smart and can get bored or frustrated with the same exercise. Short, rewarding sessions beat long, tiring ones.

What if my corgi ignores the herding ball completely?

Try a different ball size or material. Some corgis respond better to a squeaky insert, a ball with a textured surface, or a ball that already smells familiar.
